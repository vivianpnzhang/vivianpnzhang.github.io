the case

Native started in 2015 as an online, direct-to-consumer deodorant brand in San Francisco, and P&G bought it in 2017 for about US$100M. Today it sells body, hair, face, hand and oral care at Walmart, Shoppers Drug Mart, Loblaws and Amazon, built on one promise: clean, simple, effective.

The problem came through a shopper named Nadia. She loves Native and keeps the bathroom stocked for everyone at home, but her household only uses it because she buys it. Some don't know why it's worth more than cheaper brands, some think it's “a brand for women,” and most just grab whatever's already there.

As Native's brand team, we had $1M and 12 months to answer one question: how can Native become the clean personal care brand for the entire household?

our hook

This morning, on our way here, we walked past the Laurier Athletic Complex. At 9 a.m. on a Saturday, it was packed. For our generation, the gym isn't just a workout. It's where we hang out, where we post, where we decide who we are. And every one of those students walks into the locker room afterward and makes a choice: what goes on their body.

Many of them grew up with Native already in the bathroom at home. Mom bought it. But they never chose it. So we decided to meet Gen Z where they sweat.

our recommendation

We proposed a Native Wellness Community: a space for people who care about what goes on their body, powered by Native. The goal is to tie Native to wellness spaces like gyms, yoga, boxing and pilates studios, and connect members with wellness support, so Native becomes the go-to for clean personal care.

Members earn points for sharing their routines and wellness journeys through posts, blogs and comments, and redeem them for coupons, discounts and free products. Think Sephora, but for people who care about quality in personal care.

Why Gen Z? Most older generations already have a product they've loved for decades. Gen Z is still looking, and we want Native front and centre when they choose. Then they bring it home.

the plan & the $1M

sampling $300K creators $150K community $500K reserve $50K
  1. months 0–3 · $300,000

    Sample where they sweat

    Free travel-size samples at university gyms and popular gyms in the city, each with a first-purchase discount so a sample leads straight to a full-size sale. We'd also tease the Wellness Community and open early sign-ups, with a draw for 1 of 100 free full-size Native products.

  2. months 3–6 · $150,000

    Shift the mindset

    Partner with popular yoga, pilates, gym and boxing creators, with a push for more male creators, to break the idea that Native is a feminine brand. It's for anyone who cares about what goes on their body.

  3. months 6–12 · $500,000

    Launch the Native Wellness Community

    Build and promote the community: the first 1,000 members to post enter a giveaway for a year's supply of Native and a free açaí bowl membership. Then bring it home: referring family and friends earns points for both sides, so Gen Z becomes the reason the whole household chooses Native.

  4. held back · $50,000

    Reserve

    Kept aside so we can move money if sampling doesn't convert.

risk & mitigation

risk
Students take the free sample but never buy full size. They're used to cheaper brands, and Native costs more.
mitigation
Every sample has a QR code for a discount on a first full-size purchase at Shoppers or Walmart, so trying it leads straight to buying it. Our fitness creators show why it's worth more, so it's not just a freebie.
contingency
We track QR redemptions monthly. If conversion is low after 3 months, sampling money moves into creator content and in-store travel sizes, so we still reach them at a lower price point. That's why $50K stays in reserve.

how we closed

So tomorrow morning, the Laurier Athletic Complex will be packed again. The only difference? When those students walk into the locker room, they'll reach for Native. Not because Mom bought it, but because they chose it.

my first case comp

This was my first ever case competition, and I honestly didn't know what to expect walking in. Forty-five minutes to read a case, build a strategy, and plan out a million dollars felt impossible at first, and the no-slides rule made it even scarier. There was nothing to hide behind, just us, our ideas, and the judge.

What surprised me most was how fun it got once we found our story. The moment we connected the case to the packed gym we'd walked past that morning, everything clicked: the audience, the plan, the hook, the close. Presenting it felt less like reciting and more like talking to someone about an idea we really believed in.

Winning Best Speaker was such a happy surprise, and it meant a lot for a first try. I left with more confidence thinking on my feet, and I can't wait to do more of these.
